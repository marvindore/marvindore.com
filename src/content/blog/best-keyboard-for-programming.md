Let me be clear first: the best keyboard for a programmer is whatever keyboard you have. There is a programmer out there with a ten dollar keyboard who could out-code a lot of us. As long as you have a keyboard, you can put in work.

This post (and the video above) is about something a little different. When we're coding, we want a keyboard that makes us feel comfortable and lets us code flawlessly. And I'm not going to give you the usual "it depends." I know there's no one-size-fits-all, because if there were, the world would look pretty different right now.

The best keyboard for anyone is a custom one, but custom boards get pricey. That's why my pick is the **Drop ALT**. Here's why.

## What's in the box

The ALT arrived in a plain brown box, nothing fancy. Inside is a 65% modular keyboard with 67 keys instead of the usual 87, and a floating keycap design. It comes with:

- A USB-C to USB-A cable
- A keycap puller and a key switch puller
- Magnetic feet, so you can prop the board up at an angle

## Build and features

The construction is solid: an aluminum frame with very slim bezels. The standout features:

- **Hot-swappable switch sockets**, so you can change switches without any soldering
- **Fully programmable with QMK**, for macros and custom key bindings
- **RGB backlighting and underlighting**, which is a nice touch
- **Double-shot PBT keycaps**

Mine came with **Halo Clear** switches. I usually type on Cherry MX Browns, and these were really nice. They have a 65 gram actuation force, and I think they'll be my new typing switches.

## Four reasons it's my pick

### 1. It's highly customizable

If you use a keyboard every day for a living, you want it set up your way. With hot-swappable sockets you can swap any key to suit you: a heavier press on the delete key, a softer one on the space bar, mix and match however you like. Tired of Cherry MX Reds? Try Browns without paying for a whole new board. I think that's huge.

On top of that, QMK lets you create a macro for a specific key press or remap keys. You shouldn't have to buy a new board for that. Use the configurator they offer, flash the firmware, and you're good to go.

### 2. It supports every operating system

As a programmer you'll sometimes be on Windows, sometimes on a Mac, maybe on Linux. You shouldn't need a different keyboard for each. The ALT works out of the box with Windows, Linux and macOS, and you can always flash the firmware for whatever configuration you want.

### 3. It's reliable

Since I got my ALT I've had no problems at all. The build quality is great, my key presses are flawless, and I love the texture of the keys. It lets me glide across the keyboard.

I like the 65% layout because I don't use a number pad much, and the number row above the letters covers what I need. It also keeps the arrow keys, which I do use.

### 4. It teaches you about your keyboard

I had never swapped switches or taken a board apart and put it back together. The ALT encourages me to do all of that: to program macros, change key bindings, and learn how a keyboard actually works. That's a big bonus for me.

If programming your board makes you nervous, the ALT comes with a cheat sheet of the default mappings so you can find your way around as-is.
